> We now study compactness in metric spaces.

# Compactness in Metric Spaces

## Topological Properties of Metric Spaces

Let $(X,d)$ be a metric space. Its metric topology is generated by the basis

$$\mathcal B=\{B(x,r)\mid x\in X,\ r>0\}.$$

### First countability

Every metric space is first countable: for each $x\in X$,

$$\mathcal B_x=\{B(x,r)\mid r\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\}$$

is a countable neighbourhood basis at $x$. Consequently:

- $F\subseteq X$ is closed if and only if it contains the limits of all convergent sequences in $F$;
- for every topological space $Y$, a map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if and only if it is sequentially continuous.

### Hausdorff property

Every metric space is Hausdorff. Indeed, if $x\ne y$, then with
$\delta=d(x,y)/2$,

$$B(x,\delta)\cap B(y,\delta)=\varnothing.$$

Thus limits of convergent sequences are unique. Also, compact subsets of metric spaces are closed.

### Compact and sequentially compact sets are closed

**Proposition.** Every compact or sequentially compact subset of a metric space is closed.

**Proof.** A compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed. Now let $F\subseteq X$ be sequentially compact and suppose $x_n\in F$ with $x_n\to x_0$ in $X$. A subsequence $x_{n_k}$ converges to some $y\in F$. Since every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit, $x_{n_k}\to x_0$ as well. Uniqueness of limits gives $y=x_0\in F$. Hence $F$ is sequentially closed, and therefore closed because $X$ is first countable. $\square$

### Normality

**Proposition.** If $A,B$ are disjoint closed subsets of a metric space $X$, then there are disjoint open sets $U,V$ such that

$$A\subseteq U,\qquad B\subseteq V.$$

For example, the metric-space version of Urysohn's lemma gives a continuous $f:X\to[0,1]$ such that $f|_A=0$ and $f|_B=1$. Then one may take

$$U=f^{-1}(( -\infty,1/3)),\qquad V=f^{-1}((2/3,+\infty)).$$

This separation property is called **normality**.

## Metric Aspects

### Boundedness

For $A\subseteq X$, define its diameter by

$$\operatorname{diam}(A)=\sup\{d(x,y)\mid x,y\in A\}\in[0,+\infty].$$

**Proposition.** Every compact or sequentially compact subset of a metric space is bounded and closed.

If $A$ is unbounded and $x_0\in X$, then $\{B(x_0,n)\}_{n\ge1}$ is an open cover of $A$ with no finite subcover. Equivalently, one can choose $x_n\in A$ with $d(x_n,x_0)\to\infty$; this sequence has no convergent subsequence.

The converse fails in general metric spaces. Typical bounded closed but non-compact examples are:

- $(\mathbb N,d_{\mathrm{discrete}})$;
- $\mathbb R$ with bounded metric $\rho(x,y)=|x-y|/(1+|x-y|)$;
- $A=(0,1]$ as a subspace of $Y=(0,+\infty)$ with the Euclidean metric.

For the third example, $A$ is closed in $Y$ and bounded, but

$$\mathcal U=\left\{(1/n,2)\cap Y\mid n\in\mathbb N^+\right\}$$

is an open cover of $A$ without a finite subcover.

### Total boundedness

**Definition.** A metric space $(X,d)$ is **totally bounded** if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, finitely many open balls of radius $\varepsilon$ cover $X$.

Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a finite **$\varepsilon$-net** $N\subseteq X$, meaning

$$\forall y\in X\ \exists x\in N\quad d(x,y)<\varepsilon.$$

Every totally bounded space is bounded, but not conversely.

**Proposition.** A compact or sequentially compact metric space is totally bounded.

**Proof.** Compactness applied to the open cover $\{B(x,\varepsilon)\mid x\in X\}$ gives a finite subcover. For sequential compactness, if no finite family of $\varepsilon$-balls covers $X$, inductively choose $x_n$ outside $\bigcup_{i<n}B(x_i,\varepsilon)$. Then $d(x_n,x_m)\ge\varepsilon$ for $n\ne m$, so the sequence has no convergent subsequence. $\square$

### Lebesgue number lemma

**Lemma (Lebesgue number lemma).** Let $(X,d)$ be a compact metric space and let $\mathcal U$ be an open cover of $X$. There exists $\delta>0$ such that every $A\subseteq X$ with $\operatorname{diam}(A)<\delta$ is contained in some $U\in\mathcal U$.

For a compact $K\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, this says that for every relative open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ of $K$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $B(x,\delta)\cap K\subseteq U_\alpha$ for some $\alpha$, for every $x\in K$.

**Proof.** Otherwise, for each $n\ge1$ choose $C_n\subseteq X$ with $\operatorname{diam}(C_n)<1/n$, which is not contained in any member of $\mathcal U$. Pick $x_n\in C_n$. Compactness gives a subsequence $x_{n_k}\to x_0$. Choose $U\in\mathcal U$ containing $x_0$ and $\varepsilon_0>0$ with $B(x_0,\varepsilon_0)\subseteq U$. For large $k$,

$$\frac1{n_k}<\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2},\qquad d(x_{n_k},x_0)<\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}.$$

For $y\in C_{n_k}$, the triangle inequality yields $d(y,x_0)<\varepsilon_0$. Hence $C_{n_k}\subseteq U$, a contradiction. $\square$

## Completeness

### Cauchy sequences

**Definition.** A sequence $(x_n)$ in $(X,d)$ is a **Cauchy sequence** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that

$$d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon\qquad(n,m\ge N).$$

Every convergent sequence is Cauchy. The converse need not hold: $(1/n)$ is Cauchy in $(0,1)$ but has no limit there.

**Definition.** A metric space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges in that space.

Examples: $\mathbb R$ and $[0,1]$ are complete under the Euclidean metric, while $\mathbb Q$ and $(0,1)$ are not.

### Standard complete spaces

- A **Banach space** is a complete normed vector space. Examples include $L^p$ spaces ($1\le p\le\infty$, for a fixed measure space and modulo equality almost everywhere) and $C([a,b])$ with the supremum norm.
- A **Hilbert space** is a complete inner-product space. Its norm is $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Every nonempty closed convex set has a unique best approximation to each point, and the Riesz representation theorem identifies the space isometrically with its continuous dual (up to the usual complex-linearity convention).
- A **Fréchet space** is a complete Hausdorff locally convex topological vector space whose topology is induced by a countable point-separating family of seminorms $(p_n)$. One compatible complete metric is

$$d(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\frac{p_n(x-y)}{1+p_n(x-y)}.$$

### Bounded maps with the uniform metric

Let $(Y,d_Y)$ be complete and let

$$\mathcal B(X,Y)=\{f:X\to Y\mid f(X)\text{ is bounded in }Y\}.$$

For $f,g\in\mathcal B(X,Y)$,

$$d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X}d_Y(f(x),g(x))$$

is finite and defines a metric. Then $(\mathcal B(X,Y),d_\infty)$ is complete: a $d_\infty$-Cauchy sequence is pointwise Cauchy, hence has a pointwise limit in $Y$; the Cauchy estimate also proves that the limit is bounded and that convergence is uniform.

### Completion

For a metric space $(X,d)$, let $\mathcal C$ be the set of Cauchy sequences in $X$. For $(a_n),(b_n)\in\mathcal C$, the real sequence $d(a_n,b_n)$ is Cauchy, so define

$$d_{\mathcal C}((a_n),(b_n))=\lim_{n\to\infty}d(a_n,b_n).$$

This is a pseudometric. Quotient by

$$(a_n)\sim(b_n)\iff d_{\mathcal C}((a_n),(b_n))=0.$$

The quotient is a complete metric space. The map

$$x\longmapsto [(x,x,\ldots)]$$

is an isometric embedding with dense image, so the quotient is a **completion** of $X$.

More generally, a completion of $(X,d)$ is a complete metric space $(\widehat X,\widehat d)$ together with an isometric embedding $f:X\to\widehat X$ such that $\overline{f(X)}=\widehat X$. Every metric space has a completion, unique up to isometric isomorphism.

### Complete subspaces

**Proposition.** If $(X,d)$ is complete and $Y\subseteq X$ has the induced metric, then

$$(Y,d|_{Y\times Y})\text{ is complete}\iff Y\text{ is closed in }X.$$

Thus $\overline A$ is the smallest complete subspace of a complete metric space $X$ that contains $A$.

### Sequential compactness implies completeness

**Proposition.** Every sequentially compact metric space is complete.

**Proof.** Let $(x_n)$ be Cauchy. It has a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k}\to x$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ such that $d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon/2$ for $n,m\ge N$, then choose $k$ with $n_k\ge N$ and $d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon/2$. It follows that $d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. $\square$

### Completeness as absolute closedness

**Definition.** A metric space $(X,d_0)$ is **absolutely closed** if for every isometric embedding $f:(X,d_0)\to(Y,d_Y)$ into a metric space, $f(X)$ is closed in $Y$.

**Proposition.** A metric space is absolutely closed if and only if it is complete.

**Proof.** If $X$ is absolutely closed, embed it isometrically and densely into a completion $\widehat X$. Its image is closed by assumption and dense by construction, hence equals $\widehat X$; therefore $X$ is complete.

Conversely, suppose $X$ is complete and $f:X\to Y$ is an isometric embedding. If $f(x_n)\to y$ in $Y$, then $(f(x_n))$ is Cauchy, hence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy in $X$. Thus $x_n\to x\in X$, so $f(x_n)\to f(x)$. Uniqueness of limits gives $y=f(x)$. Therefore $f(X)$ is sequentially closed, hence closed in the metric space $Y$. $\square$

## Equivalent Notions of Compactness

For a subset $A\subseteq X$, always equip $A$ with the induced metric (equivalently, the subspace topology).

**Theorem.** For a metric space $(X,d)$ and $A\subseteq X$, the following are equivalent:

1. $A$ is compact.
2. $A$ is sequentially compact.
3. $A$ is limit-point compact.
4. $(A,d|_{A\times A})$ is complete and totally bounded.
5. $A$ is countably compact.

The nontrivial direction in (4) $\Rightarrow$ (2) follows by a nested-subsequence argument. Cover $X$ by finitely many balls of radius $1$, retain an infinite subsequence in one ball, then repeat using radius $1/2$, $1/3$, and so on. This produces nested infinite index sets $J_k$ with

$$i,j\in J_k\implies d(x_i,x_j)<\frac2k.$$

Choose $n_1<n_2<\cdots$ with $n_k\in J_k$. For $i,j\ge k$, we have $n_i,n_j\in J_k$, hence $d(x_{n_i},x_{n_j})<2/k$. The selected subsequence is Cauchy and therefore convergent by completeness.
