# Construction of Topologies

> [!Info] Motivation
> In the previous notes, a topological space was already given to us. Now let us ask a different question: if we start with a set, a family of maps, or a few spaces that we want to glue together, how can we construct a topology on the result?

## 1. Bases

### 1.1 Why do we need a basis?

Let us first recall a few familiar topologies. The metric topology is described by open balls; the Sorgenfrey topology by half-open intervals; the product topology by rectangles; and the topology of pointwise convergence by conditions at finitely many points.

Although these examples look different, they all follow the same pattern. A set $U$ is open when every point of $U$ lies in some standard small set $B$ that is still contained in $U$:

$$
\mathcal T_{\mathcal B}
=\left\{U\subseteq X\middle|\forall x\in U,\ \exists B\in\mathcal B,\ x\in B\subseteq U\right\}.
$$

Here $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$ is a family of subsets. For a metric topology, we may take $\mathcal B$ to be the family of all open balls.

This raises the natural question: **what must $\mathcal B$ satisfy in order for this formula to define a topology?**

The empty set causes no trouble. To make $X$ open, every point must belong to at least one member of $\mathcal B$. To deal with intersections, whenever $x\in B_1\cap B_2$, we should be able to find a smaller basis element containing $x$ and lying inside the intersection.

The picture to keep in mind is simple: basis elements are small building blocks sitting inside a larger open set.

### 1.2 The definition of a basis

> [!ABSTRACT] Definition
>
> Let $X$ be a set and let $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$. We call $\mathcal B$ a **topological basis** on $X$ if:
>
> 1. for every $x\in X$, there is $B\in\mathcal B$ with $x\in B$;
> 2. for every $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B$ and every $x\in B_1\cap B_2$, there is $B\in\mathcal B$ such that
>
>    $$
   x\in B\subseteq B_1\cap B_2.
   $$

The topology generated by the basis is

$$
\mathcal T_{\mathcal B}
=\left\{U\subseteq X\middle|\forall x\in U,\ \exists B\in\mathcal B,\ x\in B\subseteq U\right\}.
$$

Why is this a topology? Unions are handled point by point. For finite intersections, the second basis condition gives a smaller basis element around each point. There is also a more concrete description:

$$
\mathcal T_{\mathcal B}
=\left\{\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal B'}B\middle|\mathcal B'\subseteq\mathcal B\right\}.
$$

In other words, the basis elements are the building blocks, and open sets are arbitrary unions of those building blocks.

### 1.3 Different bases, the same topology

One topology can have many different bases. For the usual topology on $\mathbb R^2$, all of the following generate the same topology:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal B_1&=\{B(x,r)\mid x\in\mathbb R^2,\ r>0\},\\
\mathcal B_2&=\{B(x,r)\mid x\in\mathbb Q^2,\ r\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\},\\
\mathcal B_3&=\{(a,b)\times(c,d)\mid a<b,\ c<d\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second basis is countable. So a topology may contain many open sets while still admitting a small, manageable basis.

### 1.4 The minimality property

> [!NOTE] Theorem — Minimality of the generated topology
> Suppose $\mathcal T'$ is a topology on $X$ and $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal T'$. Since $\mathcal T'$ is closed under arbitrary unions, $\mathcal T_{\mathcal B}\subseteq\mathcal T'$.
>
> Thus the topology generated by a basis is the smallest topology containing that basis.

## 2. Subbases

Sometimes it is easier to write down a family of sets first and take finite intersections afterwards. This leads to the notion of a subbasis.

The construction is worth drawing once:

```tikz
\usepackage{amsmath}
\usepackage{amsfonts}
\usepackage{amssymb}
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=stealth]
\node[draw, rounded corners] (s1) at (-2,0) {$S_1$};
\node[draw, rounded corners] (s2) at (0,0) {$S_2$};
\node[draw, rounded corners] (b) at (2.5,0) {$B=S_1\cap S_2$};
\draw[->] (s1) -- (b);
\draw[->] (s2) -- (b);
\node at (2.5,-0.65) {basis element};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

> [!ABSTRACT] Definition
>
> Let $\mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$. Let
>
> $$
> \mathcal B_{\mathcal S}
> =\left\{S_1\cap\cdots\cap S_n\mid n\ge 1,\ S_i\in\mathcal S\right\}.
> $$
>
> If $\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal S}S=X$, then $\mathcal S$ is a **subbasis**, and the topology generated by the finite intersections above is the topology generated by $\mathcal S$.

If the subbasis does not cover $X$, we simply add $X$ to it.

> [!Example] Examples
> The following three examples show how finite intersections of a subbasis produce familiar topologies.

1. On $\mathbb R$, the rays

   $$
   (-\infty,a),\qquad(a,\infty),\qquad a\in\mathbb R
   $$

   form a subbasis for the usual topology.
2. On $X\times Y$, the family

   $$
   \{U\times Y\mid U\in\mathcal T_X\}\cup
   \{X\times V\mid V\in\mathcal T_Y\}
   $$

   is a subbasis, because

   $$
   (U\times Y)\cap(X\times V)=U\times V.
   $$
3. In the function space $M([0,1],\mathbb R)$, the evaluation maps

   $$
   \operatorname{ev}_x(f)=f(x)
   $$

   give a natural subbasis for the topology of pointwise convergence.

## 3. Product and Box Topologies

### 3.1 The product topology

For two spaces $X$ and $Y$, the rectangles

$$
U\times V,\qquad U\in\mathcal T_X,\ V\in\mathcal T_Y
$$

form a basis for the product topology.

For a family $(X_\alpha,\mathcal T_\alpha)$, the product is

$$
\prod_\alpha X_\alpha=\{(x_\alpha)\mid x_\alpha\in X_\alpha\}.
$$

A basis for the product topology consists of products

$$
\prod_\alpha U_\alpha,
$$

where $U_\alpha=X_\alpha$ for all but finitely many indices. The word “finitely” is the important part: a basic neighborhood checks only finitely many coordinates.

The product and box choices differ exactly at this point:

```tikz
\usepackage{amsmath}
\usepackage{amsfonts}
\usepackage{amssymb}
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=stealth]
\node[draw, rounded corners, align=center] (p) at (-2.4,0) {product\\finite restrictions};
\node[draw, rounded corners, align=center] (b) at (2.4,0) {box\\all restrictions};
\draw[->] (p) -- node[above] {finer} (b);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

### 3.2 The box topology

If we allow every coordinate to be restricted, we obtain the box topology. Its basic sets are

$$
\mathcal B_{\mathrm{box}}
=\left\{\prod_\alpha U_\alpha\mid U_\alpha\in\mathcal T_\alpha\right\}.
$$

Clearly,

$$
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{product}}\subseteq\mathcal T_{\mathrm{box}}.
$$

The inclusion can be strict. Consider the diagonal map

$$
f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^{\mathbb N},\qquad f(t)=(t,t,t,\ldots).
$$

Into the product topology, $f$ is continuous because every coordinate map is continuous. In the box topology, however, consider

$$
W=(-1,1)\times\left(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)\times
\left(-\tfrac13,\tfrac13\right)\times\cdots.
$$

Then $f^{-1}(W)=\{0\}$, which is not open in $\mathbb R$. Thus $f$ is not continuous into the box product.

> [!Warning] Remark
> A finer topology is not automatically a more convenient topology; it may even destroy continuity.

### 3.3 The universal property of the product

> [!NOTE] Theorem — Universal property of the product
> The product topology is the weakest topology making every projection

> $\pi_\alpha:\prod_\beta X_\beta\to X_\alpha$

> continuous. Equivalently, for any map $f:X\to\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$, continuity can be checked coordinate by coordinate.

$$
f\text{ is continuous}
\Longleftrightarrow
\pi_\alpha\circ f\text{ is continuous for every }\alpha.
$$

So when a map has values in a product, we can check continuity one coordinate at a time.

## 4. Initial Topology

Now let us reverse the direction of the previous problem. Suppose we have maps

$$
f_\alpha:X\to(X_\alpha,\mathcal T_\alpha).
$$

We want a topology on $X$ making all of them continuous. There may be many choices, so we choose the weakest one.

> [!ABSTRACT] Initial topology — Definition
>
> The **initial topology** induced by $\{f_\alpha\}$ is the smallest topology on $X$ for which every $f_\alpha$ is continuous.

It is generated by the subbasis

$$
\mathcal S_{\mathrm{init}}
=\{f_\alpha^{-1}(U)\mid U\in\mathcal T_\alpha\}.
$$

The universal property is

$$
g:Z\to X\text{ is continuous}
\Longleftrightarrow
f_\alpha\circ g:Z\to X_\alpha\text{ is continuous for every }\alpha.
$$

The word “initial” can be remembered as follows: we start with maps **out of** $X$, and pull open sets back to $X$.

### 4.1 Examples of initial topologies

> [!Example] Examples
> Metric, pointwise-convergence, and weak topologies can all be viewed as initial topologies.

1. **Metric topology.** For every $x\in X$, define

   $$
   d_x:X\to\mathbb R,\qquad d_x(y)=d(x,y).
   $$

   The initial topology induced by all these maps is the metric topology.
2. **Pointwise convergence.** Identify

   $$
   M([0,1],\mathbb R)=\mathbb R^{[0,1]}.
   $$

   The initial topology induced by all evaluation maps $\operatorname{ev}_x(f)=f(x)$ is the topology of pointwise convergence.
3. **Weak topology.** If $X$ is a normed space and $X^*$ is its continuous dual, the weak topology is the initial topology induced by all linear functionals $\ell:X\to\mathbb R$ with $\ell\in X^*$.

## 5. Final Topology

Let us now reverse the arrows once more. Suppose we have maps

$$
f_\alpha:(X_\alpha,\mathcal T_\alpha)\to Y.
$$

This time we want the strongest topology on $Y$ making all $f_\alpha$ continuous.

> [!ABSTRACT] Final topology — Definition
>
> The **final topology** induced by $\{f_\alpha\}$ is
>
> $$
> \mathcal T_{\mathrm{final}}
> =\{V\subseteq Y\mid f_\alpha^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal T_\alpha\text{ for every }\alpha\}.
> $$

Its universal property says that, for any map $g:Y\to Z$,

$$
g\text{ is continuous}
\Longleftrightarrow
g\circ f_\alpha:X_\alpha\to Z\text{ is continuous for every }\alpha.
$$

So the memory aid is:

- maps out of a space give an initial topology;
- maps into a space give a final topology.

Here is the same distinction as a picture:

The quotient topology is the most important example of this construction.

## 6. Quotient Topology

### 6.1 Quotient maps

Suppose $p:X\to Y$ is a surjection. We want to identify some points of $X$ and regard the result as a new space.

> [!ABSTRACT] Quotient topology — Definition
>
> The quotient topology on $Y$ induced by $p$ is
>
> $$
> \mathcal T_Y
> =\{V\subseteq Y\mid p^{-1}(V)\text{ is open in }X\}.
> $$

The map $p$ is called a **quotient map**. In other words, a subset of $Y$ is declared open precisely when its full preimage in $X$ is open.

### 6.2 The quotient universal property

Let $f:X\to Z$ be continuous. If $f$ is constant on the fibers of $p$, meaning

$$
p(x_1)=p(x_2)\Longrightarrow f(x_1)=f(x_2),
$$

then $f$ descends to a unique map

$$
\bar f:Y\to Z,\qquad \bar f(p(x))=f(x).
$$

> [!NOTE] Theorem — Quotient universal property
> The descended map $\bar f$ is continuous. Thus, when defining a map on a quotient, the first thing to check is whether the proposed map respects the identifications.

### 6.3 Quotients by equivalence relations

Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $X$. The quotient set is

$$
X/\sim=\{[x]\mid x\in X\},
$$

where $[x]$ denotes the equivalence class of $x$. The canonical projection

$$
p:X\to X/\sim,\qquad p(x)=[x]
$$

gives the quotient set its quotient topology.

This is the general way to make some points into one point.

For the standard example, the two endpoints of an interval become one point:

```tikz
\usepackage{amsmath}
\usepackage{amsfonts}
\usepackage{amssymb}
\begin{document}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=stealth]
\draw[thick] (-3,0) -- (-1,0);
\fill (-3,0) circle (2pt);
\fill (-1,0) circle (2pt);
\node at (-2,-0.45) {$[0,1]$};
\node at (-2,0.38) {$0\sim1$};
\draw[->] (-0.5,0) -- (0.7,0);
\draw[thick] (2,0) circle (0.9);
\node at (2,-1.25) {$S^1$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

### 6.4 Examples

> [!Example] Examples
> The following quotient spaces all come from identifying points and then using the quotient topology.

1. Identifying the endpoints of an interval gives

   $$
   [0,1]/(0\sim1)\cong S^1.
   $$

2. On $\mathbb R$, let

   $$
   x\sim y\Longleftrightarrow x-y\in\mathbb Z.
   $$

   Then $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\cong S^1$.
3. If $x\sim y$ whenever $x-y\in\mathbb Q$, the quotient topology is indiscrete. This shows that quotient spaces can be badly behaved even when the original space is very nice.
4. Real projective space is

   $$
   \mathbb{RP}^n=(\mathbb R^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\sim,
   $$

   where $x\sim y$ if and only if $y=\lambda x$ for some $\lambda\ne0$. Equivalently,

   $$
   \mathbb{RP}^n=S^n/(x\sim-x).
   $$

5. In the same way,

   $$
   \mathbb{CP}^n=(\mathbb C^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\sim,
   $$

   where $x\sim y$ if and only if $y=\lambda x$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$.

## 7. Building Spaces by Gluing

The quotient construction becomes especially visual when we use it to glue spaces together.

### 7.1 Attaching spaces

Let $A\subseteq Y$ and let $f:A\to X$ be continuous. The space obtained by attaching $Y$ to $X$ along $f$ is

$$
X\cup_fY=(X\sqcup Y)/\{a\sim f(a)\mid a\in A\}.
$$

We start with the disjoint union, then identify each $a$ with its image $f(a)$.

### 7.2 Wedge sum and smash product

If $x_0\in X$ and $y_0\in Y$ are chosen basepoints, the wedge sum is

$$
X\vee Y=(X\sqcup Y)/(x_0\sim y_0).
$$

For example, $S^1\vee S^1$ is the figure-eight space.

The smash product is

$$
X\wedge Y=(X\times Y)/(X\vee Y),
$$

where the copy of $X\vee Y$ inside $X\times Y$ is

$$
(X\times\{y_0\})\cup(\{x_0\}\times Y).
$$

In particular, $S^m\wedge S^n\cong S^{m+n}$.

### 7.3 Cone, suspension, and join

The cone on $X$ is

$$
CX=(X\times[0,1])/(X\times\{1\}),
$$

where the top face is collapsed to one point. The suspension is obtained by collapsing both boundary faces:

$$
SX=(X\times[0,1])/
\bigl(X\times\{0\}\text{ collapsed and }X\times\{1\}\text{ collapsed}\bigr).
$$

For two spaces $X$ and $Y$, their join is

$$
X*Y=(X\times Y\times[0,1])/\sim,
$$

where

$$
(x,y_1,0)\sim(x,y_2,0),
\qquad
(x_1,y,1)\sim(x_2,y,1).
$$

At time $0$ we forget the $Y$-coordinate; at time $1$ we forget the $X$-coordinate. The join of two points is an interval, and $S^m*S^n\cong S^{m+n+1}$.

### 7.4 Mapping cylinder, mapping cone, and mapping torus

For a continuous map $f:X\to Y$, the mapping cylinder is

$$
M_f=(X\times[0,1])\sqcup Y\big/((x,1)\sim f(x)).
$$

The mapping cone is obtained by collapsing the other end:

$$
C_f=(X\times[0,1])\sqcup_fY\big/(X\times\{0\}\text{ collapsed}).
$$

If $f:X\to X$, its mapping torus is

$$
T_f=(X\times[0,1])/((x,1)\sim(f(x),0)).
$$

The formulas are short, but the real content is geometric: which points have we decided to identify?

## 8. Group Actions and Orbit Spaces

### 8.1 The homeomorphism group

For a topological space $X$, let

$$
\operatorname{Homeo}(X)=\{f:X\to X\mid f\text{ is a homeomorphism}\}.
$$

Under composition, this is a group. It records the symmetries of $X$.

### 8.2 Group actions

Let $G$ be a group and $X$ a set. A left action is a map

$$
\alpha:G\times X\to X,\qquad(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x,
$$

such that

$$
e\cdot x=x,
\qquad
g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x.
$$

The orbit of $x$ is

$$
G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x\mid g\in G\}.
$$

The associated equivalence relation is

$$
x\sim y\Longleftrightarrow\exists g\in G\text{ such that }x=g\cdot y,
$$

and the orbit space is the quotient

$$
X/G=X/\sim.
$$

The quotient picture is: points move along their orbit, and the whole orbit is represented by one point downstairs.

```tikz

\draw[->] (0,1) to[bend left=35] (-1,0);
\draw[->] (-1,0) to[bend left=35] (0,-1);
\node at (0,-1.45) {$G\text{-orbit}$};
\draw[->] (1.45,0) -- (3.0,0);
\node[draw, circle] at (4.0,0) {$[x]$};
\node at (4,-0.8) {$X/G$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{document}
```

If $X$ is topological, we usually require the action maps to be homeomorphisms and give $X/G$ the quotient topology.

### 8.3 Examples

> [!Example] Examples
> Translations, rotations, and antipodal actions give standard orbit spaces.

1. $\mathbb Z$ acts on $\mathbb R$ by translations:

   $$
   n\cdot x=n+x,
   $$

   giving $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\cong S^1$.
2. $\mathbb Z_n$ acts on $S^1\subseteq\mathbb C$ by rotations:

   $$
   k\cdot z=e^{2\pi ik/n}z.
   $$

3. $\mathbb Z_2$ acts on $S^n$ by $x\mapsto-x$, giving $\mathbb{RP}^n$.
4. $\mathbb Z^n$ acts on $\mathbb R^n$ by integer translations, giving

   $$
   \mathbb R^n/\mathbb Z^n\cong(S^1)^n.
   $$

5. For coprime positive integers $p,q$, the action

   $$
   e^{2\pi ik/p}\cdot(z_1,z_2)
   =\left(e^{2\pi ik/p}z_1,e^{2\pi ikq/p}z_2\right)
   $$

   of $\mathbb Z_p$ on $S^3\subseteq\mathbb C^2$ gives the lens space

   $$
   L(p;q)=S^3/\mathbb Z_p.
   $$

### 8.4 The Hopf fibration

Finally, let us look at a beautiful example. The circle $S^1$ acts on

$$
S^3=\{(z_1,z_2)\in\mathbb C^2\mid |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1\}
$$

by scalar multiplication:

$$
z\cdot(z_1,z_2)=(zz_1,zz_2).
$$

Every orbit is a circle, and the quotient is a 2-sphere:

$$
S^3/S^1\cong S^2.
$$

To see the geometry, divide $S^3$ into

$$
X=\{(z_1,z_2)\in S^3\mid |z_1|\le|z_2|\},
\qquad
Y=\{(z_1,z_2)\in S^3\mid |z_1|\ge|z_2|\}.
$$

Both pieces are invariant under the action. Their quotients are disks, and the quotient of their common boundary is a circle. Gluing the two disks along that circle produces $S^2$.

The point is not only to remember $S^3/S^1\cong S^2$. It is to see the general pattern: turn a symmetry into an equivalence relation, and then turn that relation into a quotient space.

## Summary

The two lectures revolve around two questions.

First: **how can we generate a topology from sets or maps?** Bases and subbases describe open sets; initial topologies are the weakest topologies making maps out of a space continuous; final topologies are the strongest topologies making maps into a space continuous.

Second: **how can we glue points or spaces together?** Quotient topology gives the common language. Wedges, cones, suspensions, joins, mapping cylinders, projective spaces, orbit spaces, and lens spaces are all variations of the same operation: identify points, then use the quotient topology.

So in the end, the construction of a topology comes back to two simple questions:

> [!Question] Two guiding questions
> Which local sets should count as open?
>
> Which points should count as the same point?
